Before we begin

Mario is on a train. His phone shows a p3 session in which the agent is writing a long reply. Every token is a small state change: the worker commits it, the server relays it, the phone applies it. The train enters a tunnel and the phone's link drops to a trickle, 64 KB per second. The worker keeps producing tokens at full speed.

Without flow control, every token still gets sent. The bytes pile up somewhere between the worker and the phone, and when the train leaves the tunnel the phone has to work through a backlog of old tokens before it sees the current text. It is not only slow: what it shows is minutes old, and nothing tells it so.

This note explains what keeps the phone current, why the obvious answer (let the transport push back) fails, and the design Astra and Fable converged on after measuring it.

Six things to know

  1. Transport backpressure arrives too late. The kernel's and a proxy's buffers take megabytes before a WebSocket reports anything buffered; the phone sees state 4 to 10 seconds old.
  2. Acknowledgements keep state fresh. The receiving application acknowledges each message; at most two are in flight; the newest state was 0.0 seconds old on the same 64 KB/s link.
  3. Count messages, not bytes. Each message carries everything that piled up since the last acknowledgement, so throughput adapts to the link by itself.
  4. Merge ops first, snapshot last. 2,000 merged token appends cost 26 KB, sent one by one 46 KB; a snapshot of a member with 500 KB of history would cost 508 KB.
  5. Calls need their own window. A tab firing 100,000 calls pushed another client's p95 to 345 ms; with a window of 64 respected by the caller it stayed at 0.66 ms and the flood waited in the buggy tab.
  6. Nothing is refused for being too many (decision K16): extra state is merged, extra calls wait.

Words used throughout

A link connects two hosts. The provider owns an object and sends its state; the consumer holds a reference to it. A hold is one reference over a link, with its own stream of ops (Delta's changes). A snapshot is a hold's whole state. An acknowledgement (ack) is a tiny message the consumer sends after it applied a state message. In flight means sent and not yet acknowledged. Coalescing means merging the ops that wait to be sent. A relay is a host in the middle (the server between worker and tab).

I

Where the Bytes Hide

Why the sender cannot see a slow phone.

A WebSocket in Node reports bufferedAmount: bytes the library still holds. It looks like the perfect signal: stop sending when it grows. The experiment says otherwise.

Flood, ~9,800 × 128 B per second, 64 KB/s link Sender memory Newest state the phone saw
no flow control 7.0 MB queued in the WebSocket, heap +18.5 MB 9.4 s old
cap bufferedAmount at 64 KiB, merge pending state +0.46 MB still 9.4 s old
cap at 1 MiB (Fable's rerun) small 3.8 s, growing to 10.4 s
at most 1 to 4 acknowledged messages in flight, merge pending state +0.4 MB 0.0 s

The transport's backpressure protects the sender's memory, but freshness depends on buffers the sender cannot see: the operating system's socket buffer, autotuned to hundreds of kilobytes, and every proxy on the way. A normal streamed reply (50 tokens per second, 23 bytes each) never fills them; a burst or a tunnel does. Only the receiving application knows what it has applied.

II

Acknowledged Messages

A window of two messages in flight, and why it adapts to any link.

The rule: a provider has at most K = 2 state messages in flight per link. The consumer sends ack (three bytes in the binary codec) after it applied one. While two are in flight, new commits do not produce messages; their ops wait, merged per hold, and go out in the next message once an ack arrives.

Two messages in flight on a slow link

Fast link

Commits 1, 2, 3 each become a message; each is acknowledged before the next commit. Nothing waits: the window never fills, so a normal stream behaves exactly as without flow control.

The link slows

Messages 1 and 2 are in flight. Commits 3 to 400 arrive; their ops are folded into the pending queue of each member they touch (chapter III). Memory: the net change, not the history; at most 10,000 ops per member.

An ack arrives

ack for message 1. The provider sends message 3, carrying the folded ops of commits 3 to 400, encoded once. The phone applies them as one batch and shows the newest text.

Self-clocking

The slower the link, the more each message carries; the faster, the smaller and more frequent. Nobody measures bandwidth: the acknowledgements pace the sender.

The simulation above shows the same effect in miniature. Drag the link below the commit rate: the upper lane's queue grows without bound and the phone falls further behind every second; the lower lane never has more than two messages waiting, and the phone is at most a message behind.

III

Fold the Backlog

What a waiting hold keeps while the window is full, and why it never measures sizes.

While messages are in flight, each hold member keeps the ops of new commits in a pending queue and folds them: a new op merges into the last pending op on the same path when that is safe, so a backlog keeps the net change, not the history. When an acknowledgement frees a slot, the queue is encoded once into one message.

Last pending op New op Kept
a text "Hel" a text "lo" one a text "Hello"
s count 41 or an append s count 42 the s: it overwrites what came before
anything r value only the r: a replacement makes everything before it moot
anything on a path in the index a structural op (p, m, d, t, s of an object) appended; the index is cleared

The merge partner is found through a leaf index: a map from path to the last pending append or primitive set on that path. Leaf ops on different paths commute, so a new op may merge into an earlier one, not only into its neighbour; a status field changing between two tokens no longer stops the reply from folding. Any structural op clears the index, because after a splice entries[7] may name another entry. Past 10,000 pending ops the queue keeps only "replace with the current value", which then costs nothing while the consumer stays slow.

Why no size rule. The first idea was to send a snapshot once the merged ops outgrow the member. The reviewers measured it on real p2 data and it failed three ways: "snapshot bytes plus sent op bytes" is no bound (an interned path lets a value grow faster than its ops: 5,404 B of snapshot after 832 B of ops), it drifts 1,254 times too high under churn, and an exact size needs a size on every node of the value. Folding alone came within about 10% of an exact oracle, with no measuring at all.

60 s stall, all workloads Uncompressed Compressed
merged ops, no folding 466 KB 166 KB
adjacent folding 399 KB 147 KB
leaf index (the decision) 372 KB 145 KB
exact oracle (not buildable) 335 KB 136 KB

One queue for every slow consumer. The same queue serves Chord's shared watch handle: a listener still busy with the last frame gets the commits made meanwhile as one call with the newest value and the folded ops, instead of today's "after 100 frames, replace everything". With a listener taking one second, a conversation view's watch went from 402 calls and 17.9 MB of ops to 312 calls and 257 KB. Durable's event stream keeps a queue of its own, because events are not state: folding would drop them.

IV

Calls Get Their Own Window

Why acknowledged state does not protect against a call flood.

Calls are small and the provider acknowledges nothing until they finish, so a byte or message window on state says nothing about how many handlers run. A tab with a bug:

for (const note of notes) ref.add(note, ctx);   // 100,000 calls, none awaited
100,000 calls, handlers awaiting 10 ms Finished in Provider memory Another client's p95 round trip
no limit 0.66 s +56.5 MB, 43,824 running 129 ms (345 ms with trivial handlers)
provider-side semaphore of 64 15.9 s +42.5 MB, 96,736 queued on the provider 0.38 ms
caller-side window of 64 17.1 s +4 MB, nothing queued on the provider 0.66 ms
handlers doing 10 ms of CPU work blocked about 1 s, with or without a limit

The provider's hello announces a call window N (256 by default) per origin (chapter VI). A consumer keeps at most N of its own calls unanswered per link; further calls wait in the link's call queue, in order. Nothing is refused (K16); a peer that sends more than N is a protocol error. The flood waits in the tab that caused it. CPU-heavy handlers block the event loop whatever the window: that stays the handler author's responsibility.

V

Relays

One link to a worker carries many tabs. Decided: flow control per origin.

The server relays every tab's holds to a session's worker over one link. Three ways to apply the acknowledged window across that hop were considered:

How (c) works:

  • Origins. The relay gives each downstream link a number, never reused; every message upstream carries it (origin), and the worker keys holds, calls, windows and acks by (link, origin, id). Ids are not renumbered.
  • What the relay keeps. Per relayed hold its upstream link and its original hold message (to route calls and hold again after a worker restart); per tab a FIFO of which worker sent each message in flight, so each in-order ack goes back to the right worker. Nothing is parsed, folded, dropped or reset.
  • Calls. N is per origin at the worker; the relay forwards calls at once, each tab bounded by its own window.
  • A tab leaves. Its link closes; the relay sends closed { origin } to each worker it relayed to, which releases that origin's holds and aborts its calls.
  • Cost. A tab served by W workers may have 2W messages in flight (plus the relay's own 2). Two bytes of origin per message on the worker link.

What Changes in the Design

The converged scheme, what it replaces, and the relay decision.

Converged (Astra and Fable, after cross-checking each other's experiments): at most K = 2 acknowledged state messages in flight; pending ops folded per hold member through a leaf index, capped at 10,000; a call window N announced in hello, extra calls queued by the caller; holds unlimited; size limits stay a separate check.

Replaced from the design (§3, §22): the byte window and its credit messages, snapshot admission and debt (R19, Q3), cut C12's call-credit accounting, any adapter backlog API. IPC and WebSocket behave the same, because acknowledgements are messages.

Relays (owner): per-hop acknowledgements (b) were decided on 2026-10-23 and built in slice 4a; the benchmarks showed a slow tab recovering through resets far worse than a direct tab (24 s and three snapshots after a 1 s stall). On 2026-10-24 they were replaced by flow control per origin (c): the worker keeps window, pending queues and calls per (link, origin), the relay only forwards and returns acks. Measured after the rework: a slow tab through the relay catches up exactly like a direct one. The circuit breaker stayed undecided; the heartbeat (15 s ping, 30 s close) was built instead.
